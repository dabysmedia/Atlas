
### Core Setting

A newly accessible landmass has been discovered across previously impassable seas.

For generations, sailors knew that something existed beyond the ______

Recently, the seas have become navigable—whether because of ______

(IE: changing currents, new technology, magic, or some unexplained natural phenomenon.)

What explorers found was an enormous tropical island or isolated continent covered almost entirely in rainforest.

For now, foreigners simply call it something like The New World, though every native civilization has its own name for it.

The land appears to contain almost unimaginable wealth.

Rare metals.

Gemstones.

Exotic hardwoods.

Medicinal plants.

Spices and dyes.

Strange animals.

Ancient artifacts.

Possibly resources that do not exist anywhere else in the known world.

Early expeditions returning with even small quantities of these materials created fortunes overnight.

The discovery has triggered an international land rush.

The great nations of the old world cannot openly invade without risking war with one another.

Instead, they issue royal, republican, or imperial charters to private companies.

These companies function somewhere between:

trading corporations,

mercenary armies,

exploration societies,

colonial governments,

and mining/logging operations.

They are technically private organizations, but everyone understands that they represent the interests of their home nations.

Each chartered company has broad authority.

Establish settlements.

Build forts and harbors.

Negotiate treaties.

Hire mercenaries.

Claim territory.

Extract resources.

Conduct scientific expeditions.

Trade with native peoples.

Defend themselves against rival companies.

In some cases, they may even be permitted to wage proxy war.

The race for the island’s wealth is rapidly becoming a private war.

chartered companies are not just competing for favorable trade routes and resource claims. They are actively undermining, infiltrating, and attacking one another in an escalating struggle for control of the island’s most valuable discoveries.

Maps are deliberately falsified, altered, or stolen to misdirect rival expeditions away from mineral deposits, ruins, harbors, and mountain passes.

Survey teams vanish in the interior, sometimes victims of the jungle or native resistance, but increasingly because rival companies have ambushed, bribed, captured, or murdered them.

Ships are sabotaged in harbor, supply depots are burned, bridges are destroyed, and river routes are blocked to delay competing expeditions.

Companies employ spies, scouts, informants, saboteurs, assassins, mercenaries, and defectors to steal intelligence and cripple their rivals from within.

Native alliances are bought with weapons, medicine, gold, promises of protection, or support against traditional enemies, turning old local rivalries into proxy conflicts.

Prospectors and soldiers routinely cross disputed boundaries, establishing unauthorized camps and fortified claims before rival companies can respond.

Armed clashes between company forces are increasingly common in the interior, ranging from brief ambushes and nighttime raids to open battles involving dozens or even hundreds of mercenaries.

Prisoners are sometimes exchanged or ransomed, but less scrupulous companies simply execute captured rivals and blame their deaths on hostile natives, disease, or jungle predators.

Companies raid one another’s caravans, seize excavation sites, steal recovered artifacts, and occasionally wipe out entire expeditions if the prize is valuable enough.

False-flag attacks are common. A company might massacre a rival patrol using native weapons, disguise mercenaries as another nation’s troops, or attack its own outpost to justify retaliation.

Bribery and infiltration are often more effective than open warfare. Quartermasters are paid to spoil supplies, guides deliberately lead expeditions into dangerous territory, and company officers secretly sell maps and intelligence to the highest bidder.

Some companies maintain unofficial “black divisions” whose only purpose is espionage, sabotage, assassination, and deniable military action.

Pirate crews and privateers operate along the coast under quiet corporate sponsorship, attacking rival shipping while allowing governments to deny involvement.

Colonial settlements themselves have become hotbeds of espionage. Taverns, warehouses, trading houses, and docks are filled with informants, hired muscle, smugglers, and agents watching every newly arrived expedition.

Discoveries are often kept secret even from a company’s own government until the site can be secured. A newly discovered ruin, gold deposit, or magical artifact can trigger an immediate race as competing factions mobilize forces to seize it first.

Temporary alliances form constantly. Two companies may cooperate against a stronger rival one month and begin killing each other the next.

Some chartered companies still attempt to operate within treaties and accepted rules of engagement. Others have effectively become private armies that will employ any method necessary to secure an advantage.

Everyone understands that the island is becoming a testing ground for the great powers of the world. For now, governments can dismiss the violence as disputes between private companies, pirates, and explorers.

But if one company gains overwhelming control of the island’s resources—or uncovers something powerful enough to change the balance of power—the shadow war could become a genuine international conflict.


## Geography of the Island

The island is enormous and overwhelmingly tropical.

Dense rainforest covers most of the lowlands.

Visibility away from established trails can be less than a few dozen feet.

Rainstorms are frequent and violent.

Rivers flood suddenly.

Heat, insects, disease, and humidity make expeditions exhausting.

A massive mountain range dominates the interior.

The range forms a natural wall through the center of the island.

Peaks disappear into clouds.

Few navigable passes exist.

Traveling directly across the island is extremely difficult.

Costal positions are valuable

The mountains divide the island into distinct regions.

Different climates.

Different ecosystems.

Different native cultures.

Potentially entirely different species.

Rumors suggest enormous valleys or plateaus exist among the mountains that no foreign expedition has reached.

The coastline contains several natural beaches and a handful of excellent harbors.

These harbors immediately become strategically important.

Whoever controls them controls trade and reinforcement.

Early settlements therefore develop around:

deep-water bays,

river mouths,

natural coves,

defensible cliffs.

Major rivers act as highways into the interior.

Boats are often much faster than cutting trails through jungle.

Colonial forts appear along rivers.

Native settlements often control important crossings and waterways.

Waterfalls, rapids, and seasonal flooding prevent explorers from simply sailing everywhere.

The interior remains largely unmapped.

Maps might literally contain enormous blank areas.

Expedition reports frequently contradict one another.

Entire valleys, ruins, lakes, and settlements remain undiscovered.


## Chartered Company: Mendoza

A Spanish-inspired colonial power pursuing religious expansion, territorial conquest, permanent settlement, and conversion.

The golden fortress and radiant sun of Casa de Mendoza


### Faction Profile

Nation Inspiration: Spain

Role in the Colonial Race: Religious expansion, territorial conquest, permanent settlement, and conversion.

General Reputation: Zealous, disciplined, proud, and quick to answer resistance with force.


### Identity

Casa de Mendoza sees the expedition as more than a commercial venture.

They believe they have been given a sacred opportunity to bring order, faith, and civilization to an untouched part of the world.

Wealth matters, but it is secondary to establishing lasting dominion.

They are not interested in temporary camps or quick extraction. Wherever Mendoza settles, they intend to remain.

Their worldview is intensely religious.

They believe successful expeditions reflect divine approval.

Conversion is considered both a religious obligation and a political tool.

Priests and missionaries commonly accompany soldiers and settlers.

Spreading their faith is inseparable from expanding the influence of the Crown.

They negotiate only from a position of certainty.

Native groups willing to trade, cooperate, or convert may receive protection and favorable treatment.

Groups that resist are treated as obstacles to a divine mission, and commanders are quick to escalate disputes into armed conflict.

Their attitude is simple: the cross is offered first, but the sword is never far behind it.

Faith, infrastructure, and force arrive together


### Goals

Establish permanent colonies throughout the island.

Convert native populations to their faith.

Secure strategically important harbors, river crossings, and fertile territory.

Build roads, forts, missions, and settlements intended to last generations.

Claim territory in the name of the Crown before rival companies can establish themselves.

Bring large portions of the island under a unified colonial administration.

Prevent rival nations from gaining enough influence to challenge their territorial claims.


### Crest, Colors, and Livery

Crest: A golden fortress beneath a radiant sun on a crimson shield.

The fortress represents civilization and permanent conquest; the sun symbolizes divine guidance and the spreading of their faith.

Crossed swords or spears represent the willingness to defend that mission through force.

Primary Colors: Crimson, ivory, and gold.

Settlements and camps make heavy use of red banners and gold religious symbols.

Officers wear crimson or dark red coats with gold trim; soldiers favor practical white or cream uniforms accented with red.

Senior commanders, priests, and elite soldiers may wear polished breastplates or ceremonial armor.

Architecture favors whitewashed stone, dark wood, red tile, religious statuary, and prominent banners. Even temporary camps contain a chapel or shrine.


### Motto

“Faith. Crown. Glory.”

Formal variation: “By Faith We Carry the Light.”


### Military Doctrine

Casa de Mendoza favors decisive force.

They would rather destroy an enemy’s ability to resist than fight a prolonged campaign; commanders believe hesitation invites rebellion.

Their typical pattern is to arrive, fortify, demand recognition, offer terms, and crush serious resistance.

They make heavy use of infantry formations, muskets, cannon, cavalry where terrain permits, fortified positions, and religious or magical support.

They are comfortable fighting open battles and are particularly dangerous when defending established territory.

Their aggression can create enemies faster than they can defeat them.


### Religion and Magic

Magic is accepted when it can be reconciled with Mendoza religious doctrine.

Healing magic, wards, protection rituals, blessings, and battlefield magic are widely accepted.

Mages operate under close institutional or religious oversight.

They deeply distrust uncontrolled magic, native spirit practices, forbidden experimentation, summoning, flesh-shaping, and corruptive or heretical artifacts.

Their first reaction to ancient discoveries may be to purify, seal, or destroy them rather than study them.

This places Mendoza directly at odds with Company DuPont and more magically ambitious factions.


### Technology

Mendoza specializes in technologies that support permanent occupation: fortification engineering, artillery, roads, bridges, mills, wells and irrigation, ship repair, heavy construction, and organized military logistics.

Other companies may explore faster, but few establish permanent infrastructure as quickly as Mendoza.


### Native Relations

Mendoza believes native peoples can become part of its colonial order.

Cooperative communities may receive protection, trade, education, and access to foreign technology; conversion dramatically improves their standing.

Native auxiliaries and allied communities may eventually fight alongside Mendoza troops.

Mendoza has little patience for cultures that reject its authority, and practices deemed heretical can trigger immediate conflict.

The Nhal’Kesh are likely to horrify Mendoza missionaries; blood rituals and human sacrifice may be treated as proof of corruption and used to justify destroying sacred sites.

The Aelari may initially receive better treatment because they appear peaceful and diplomatic, though conflict becomes inevitable when expansion threatens sacred land.

The Canopy People are difficult to conquer because their settlements resist conventional territorial occupation.


### Strengths

Highly disciplined soldiers.

Strong morale and ideological unity.

Excellent fortifications and engineers.

Effective artillery.

Strong religious institutions.

Willingness to commit significant manpower to permanent settlements.

Very decisive leadership.


### Weaknesses

Religious arrogance.

Rigid hierarchy.

Aggressive responses to resistance.

Difficulty understanding cultures they regard as spiritually inferior.

A tendency to create insurgencies through excessive force.

Expensive permanent settlements and military infrastructure.

Poor willingness to retreat or abandon claimed territory.

A belief in their divine mission that can cause commanders to ignore obvious warnings.


### Relationship With Rival Companies

Mendoza considers many other companies morally weak or overly concerned with profit.

Company DuPont frustrates them because its scholars often preserve and study things Mendoza believes should be destroyed.

Merchant companies are useful trading partners but are considered unreliable and spiritually hollow.

Magic-focused factions draw increasing suspicion depending on their practices.

Mendoza can cooperate with almost anyone temporarily, but ultimately believes the island should fall under its own political and religious order.


### Reputation

“Mendoza arrives with a priest, an engineer, and a cannon—and only one of them is there to ask permission.”


## Chartered Company: Company DuPont

A French-inspired scholarly power pursuing archaeology, prestige, cultural acquisition, and the recovery of rare physical relics.

The book, astrolabe, fleur-de-lis, and laurels of Company DuPont


### Faction Profile

Nation Inspiration: France

Role in the Colonial Race: Archaeology, prestige, scholarship, cultural acquisition, and the recovery of rare physical relics.

General Reputation: Cultured, brilliant, meticulous, ambitious, and deeply convinced that they understand the island better than everyone else.


### Identity

Company DuPont sees the island as the greatest intellectual and cultural discovery of the age.

They value things that can be studied, cataloged, displayed, and brought home more than flags or ordinary resources.

Ancient ruins, inscriptions, statues, ceremonial objects, artwork, rare specimens, and historical records are enormously valuable to them.

Prestige matters almost as much as profit. They would rather return with one discovery that changes history than ten wagons of ore.

Their worldview is shaped by Enlightenment-style confidence.

They believe the unknown can be measured, the mysterious classified, the ancient understood, and the supernatural eventually explained.

Their genuine curiosity is often inseparable from arrogance.

They consider themselves more civilized than the cruder colonial powers.

Officers criticize excessive violence, religious fanaticism, and careless destruction.

They rarely question their right to remove artifacts, excavate sacred sites, or decide what deserves preservation.

Their exploitation is usually wrapped in scholarship and good manners.

Discovery, documentation, preservation—and possession


### Goals

Locate and document ancient ruins.

Recover historically significant artifacts.

Acquire artwork, manuscripts, sculptures, maps, and ceremonial objects.

Catalogue native languages, customs, flora, fauna, and architecture.

Build the most complete scholarly record of the island.

Secure exclusive access to important archaeological sites.

Enhance the prestige of sponsoring universities, museums, and noble patrons.

Prevent rivals from destroying, stealing, or monopolizing discoveries.

Gain privileged access to the buried mage city and its physical remnants.


### Crest, Colors, and Livery

Crest: A golden fleur-de-lis above an open book, framed by laurel branches and an astrolabe.

The fleur-de-lis represents national prestige and patronage; the book represents scholarship and recorded knowledge.

The astrolabe represents exploration, measurement, and navigation; laurel symbolizes achievement and intellectual victory.

Primary Colors: Deep navy, ivory, and gold.

Officers wear tailored navy coats with gold trim; scholars and naturalists favor ivory or cream field coats suited to the heat.

Military personnel use blue uniforms with polished brass fittings, while senior leaders dress more elegantly than the environment reasonably allows.

Their orderly camps contain specimen crates, survey tables, canvas laboratories, portable libraries, display cases, drafting stations, and organized archives.


### Motto

“Knowledge Is the Greatest Treasure.”

Formal variation: “To Discover Is to Possess Eternity.”


### Military Doctrine

DuPont prefers precision over brute force.

Their soldiers primarily protect scholars, excavation teams, and valuable discoveries; unnecessary battles are avoided.

When force is required, they favor carefully planned engagements, defensible positions, and disciplined small units.

Their forces rely on trained skirmishers, marksmen, engineers, light artillery, scouts, and specialized expedition guards.

They are more likely to hire guides or negotiate passage than march through hostile territory.

They will withdraw from a poor position and return with better information.

Their greatest military weakness is hesitation: while DuPont gathers information, aggressive rivals may seize the objective.


### Scholarship and Magic

DuPont is fascinated by magic primarily as a historical, archaeological, and academic field of study.

They seek to understand ancient magical practice, its artifacts, and the development of magical traditions.

Expeditions commonly include historians, linguists, archaeologists, naturalists, cartographers, arcane scholars, conservators, and translators.

DuPont mages are researchers more often than battlefield specialists.

They identify enchantments, preserve artifacts, translate magical inscriptions, detect magical residues, and safely handle unknown objects.

They are especially interested in enchanted objects, spellbooks, tablets, ritual implements, magical architecture, preserved laboratories, and physical evidence of ancient magic.

Unlike more aggressive magical factions, DuPont values understanding ancient power more than immediately wielding it.


### Technology

DuPont brings technology designed to observe, document, preserve, and transport discoveries.

Their equipment includes precision surveying instruments, improved compasses, navigation tools, printing presses, drafting tables, field microscopes, botanical and chemical laboratories, preservation chemicals, artifact cases, archaeological tools, and specialized transport crates.

They are often the first faction to produce accurate maps of newly explored regions.


### Native Relations

DuPont generally prefers diplomacy and cultural study over conquest.

They learn native languages, hire interpreters and guides, and may spend years studying local customs and history.

This does not make them egalitarian: scholars can be patronizing and may treat living cultures as subjects rather than political equals.

They often assume an object is safer and better preserved in a foreign museum than with the people who created it.

Their greatest conflicts arise from archaeology: sacred burials become excavations, ceremonial objects become museum pieces, and forbidden ruins become irresistible research sites.

The Aelari may find them easier to negotiate with than Mendoza, while resenting how they intellectualize sacred traditions.

The Nhal’Kesh consider DuPont archaeologists particularly dangerous because curiosity draws them repeatedly toward sealed and deliberately forgotten places.


### Strengths

Exceptional scholars and researchers.

Excellent cartographers.

Strong university and academic connections.

Superior record keeping.

Skilled linguists and translators.

Strong archaeological expertise.

Effective artifact preservation.

Good diplomatic instincts.

Careful planning.

High-quality intelligence from exploration and research.


### Weaknesses

Intellectual arrogance.

A tendency toward analysis paralysis.

Internal rivalry among scholars, patrons, and expedition leaders.

Reluctance to destroy dangerous discoveries.

Underestimation of threats that do not fit their theories.

Poor response to rapidly changing crises.

Enormous expedition costs.

A desire to preserve and study everything, even when it should simply be left alone.


### Relationship With Rival Companies

DuPont considers Casa de Mendoza dangerously destructive and intellectually backward.

Mendoza sees forbidden relics where DuPont sees irreplaceable historical evidence; the factions can clash violently over ruins Mendoza wants purified or destroyed.

Merchant companies frustrate DuPont by breaking apart, selling, or repurposing artifacts without appreciating their historical value.

More magically aggressive factions worry them for the opposite reason: DuPont wants to understand ancient magic while others seek to harness it immediately.

They can cooperate with almost any rival when a discovery is significant enough, but usually insist on controlling the research and documentation.


### Reputation

“If you want to know what it is, ask DuPont. If you want to know whether you should touch it, ask someone else.”


## Chartered Company — The Qadir Ascendancy

An Ottoman- and broader Middle-Eastern-inspired imperial power seeking arcane infrastructure, ley lines, spirit pacts, and living magical systems.

The ordered star and hidden crescent of the Qadir Ascendancy


### Faction Profile

Nation Inspiration: Ottoman and broader Middle Eastern imperial powers

Role in the Colonial Race: Arcane power, magical infrastructure, control of ley lines, spirit pacts, and the acquisition of living magical systems rather than physical relics.

General Reputation: Sophisticated, patient, dangerous, deeply knowledgeable about magic, and willing to bargain with forces other factions avoid.


### Identity

The Qadir Ascendancy believes the island’s greatest wealth is magic itself—not gold, relics, or territory.

They seek the forces that make the island unusually magical rather than merely hauling statues and manuscripts home.

Their expeditions search for ley lines, magical springs, spirit-haunted places, enchanted minerals, reality distortions, and living beings with unusual magical abilities.

A ruined temple matters only if it still sits on something powerful.

They view magic as a resource, a science, and a political weapon.

Magic should be studied, negotiated with, controlled, and ultimately made to serve the state.

Their culture is accustomed to complexity and compromise.

They can govern diverse peoples, religions, and traditions so long as those groups accept Qadir authority.

This makes them more flexible than Mendoza and sometimes less patronizing than DuPont, but their tolerance is pragmatic rather than altruistic.

Where the world bends, Qadir builds an instrument around it


### Goals

Identify and control the island’s major sources of magical power.

Map ley lines and areas of magical instability.

Establish fortified arcane observatories and ritual sites.

Recruit or coerce native magical practitioners into Qadir service.

Secure spirit pacts, magical contracts, and access to supernatural entities.

Study the environmental effects surrounding the mage city.

Learn how ancient magical systems function at a fundamental level.

Prevent rivals from monopolizing the island’s magical resources.

Create a network of controlled magical sites capable of projecting Qadir power across the island.


### Crest, Colors, and Livery

Crest: A golden eight-pointed star surrounding a black crescent, set over a deep emerald field.

The star represents ordered knowledge and the structure of magic; the crescent represents mystery, hidden power, and the unseen world.

A thin ring of arcane script and geometric patterning surrounds the symbol; senior officers and arcanists incorporate specialized sigils.

Primary Colors: Emerald green, black, gold, and deep teal.

Officers wear structured coats or robes with gold geometric embroidery; soldiers use practical dark uniforms with green sashes and polished brass fittings.

Arcane specialists wear layered robes patterned with mathematical, astronomical, or sigil-like designs.

Their outposts feature geometric banners, brass lanterns, tiled interiors, domed command tents, carved screens, astrological instruments, and ritual circles worked into floors and courtyards.


### Motto

“Power Is Found Where the World Bends.”

Formal variation: “Know the Hidden. Bind the Unseen.”


### Military Doctrine

The Qadir Ascendancy prefers control over destruction.

They isolate enemies, restrict movement, disrupt morale, and manipulate terrain rather than charge directly.

Commanders favor layered defenses, artillery, disciplined infantry, and magical support.

Their forces use organized musket infantry, artillery, engineers, cavalry, arcanists, ward-makers, and battlefield controllers.

They excel at preparing ground with wards, trapped approaches, obscured sightlines, magical barriers, and controlled choke points.

Less impulsive than Mendoza and less cautious than DuPont, they will retreat when it creates a stronger magical or strategic position later.


### Magic

Magic is central to Qadir identity and is integrated into government, military planning, architecture, medicine, and engineering.

Their trained arcanists are among the most respected specialists on the island.

They specialize in ley lines, binding, wards, spirit contracts, protection, magical architecture, summoning, divination, and channeling ambient magical energy.

Unlike DuPont, they value artifacts mainly for reproducible magical effects; anything else is decoration.

They will dismantle an ancient site to expose the system beneath it.

They are the faction most willing to communicate with dangerous supernatural entities—not in reverence, but in negotiation.


### Technology

Qadir technology emphasizes the intersection of engineering and magic.

It includes precision astronomical instruments, magical surveying equipment, brass mechanisms, portable observatories, hydraulic engineering, irrigation systems, arcane meters, ward-inscribed artillery positions, and specialized containment vessels.

Their engineers adapt infrastructure exceptionally well to difficult environments.

Their outposts may be smaller than Mendoza colonies, but are often more technically sophisticated.


### Native Relations

The Qadir Ascendancy takes native magical traditions seriously.

They want to learn what works, why it works, and whether it can be incorporated into Qadir systems.

They may offer protection, trade, magical instruction, medicine, or political recognition for access to sacred sites and knowledge.

Their central flaw is treating sacred relationships as formal systems: a revered spirit becomes a contract partner, a grove an arcane node, and a forbidden site a research opportunity.

The Aelari may initially welcome their seriousness before recoiling from their desire to bind and control.

The Nhal’Kesh may see them as dangerously close to repeating the ancient mages’ mistakes.

The Flesh-Shapers may regard them as fascinating potential partners.


### Strengths

Highly developed magical expertise.

Excellent battlefield control.

Strong engineers.

Skilled negotiators.

Comfort with supernatural phenomena.

Effective warding and defensive magic.

Strong understanding of magical geography.

Flexible administration.

Ability to integrate foreign techniques into their systems.


### Weaknesses

Overconfidence in their ability to control magic.

Willingness to negotiate with dangerous entities.

Constant temptation to push experiments too far.

A tendency to exploit sacred traditions as systems.

Expensive specialists and equipment.

Rivalry between traditionalists and aggressive innovators.

The risk of magical disasters that are difficult to contain.

A willingness to recognize danger and still decide the reward is worth it.


### Relationship With Rival Companies

Company DuPont is their natural intellectual rival.

DuPont preserves and catalogues objects; Qadir seeks the power that made those objects possible. DuPont may excavate a temple while Qadir purchases the spring beneath it.

Casa de Mendoza distrusts Qadir practices as near-heresy, while Qadir considers Mendoza’s destruction of unknown magic wasteful and ignorant.

Merchant companies are useful sources of rare components, specialists, and logistical support.

The Ascendancy rarely allows outsiders access to its most important magical discoveries.


### Reputation

“DuPont will ask what the ruin was. Mendoza will ask whether it should exist. Qadir will ask what happens if they turn it back on.”

## The Mage City Within the Mountain


### The Precursor Mages — Chronological Summary


#### I. The College Before the City

The civilization now remembered as the Precursor Mages began around the equivalent of the ancient world's second millennium BC, roughly 2000 BC. At its heart was not originally a kingdom, empire, or even conventional city, but an extraordinary College of Mages.

The College gathered an unprecedented concentration of magical scholarship in one location. Its inhabitants were primarily concerned with magical study rather than politics, conquest, or administration. The surrounding world contained its own kingdoms and empires, but the College had little interest in ruling them.

The enormous city that would eventually surround the College was never really planned.

It accumulated.

The scholars needed food, so bakeries and other businesses appeared to provide it. The bakers needed somewhere to live. Buildings required construction workers. The construction workers needed food, lodging, entertainment, tools, and families of their own. Merchants arrived. Craftspeople followed.

Originally, mages themselves could use magic to perform much of the work necessary to maintain their community. A mage could move stone or erect a structure, for example. But using the time of an accomplished scholar to perform something as mundane as constructing a bakery increasingly seemed wasteful.

The College therefore delegated.

And every act of delegation created another small piece of the city.

Over generations, the College became the center of an enormous, decentralized campus that simply never stopped growing. Eventually, millions of people lived around it.

Most of these people were not mages.


#### II. The Accidental Government

The College never deliberately decided to govern a city.

Instead, its bureaucracy expanded whenever some practical problem became too large or tedious for individual scholars to handle.

People were appointed to administer particular responsibilities. Others acquired authority over increasingly large areas of infrastructure or commerce. Technocrats appeared with authority over narrow scopes of varying importance.

Over time, this produced something resembling a government—but without anyone actually governing it.

The College possessed administrators responsible for countless individual functions, but little effective centralized authority. These officials could be powerful within their jurisdictions without possessing the authority to coordinate the entire system.

This arrangement worked remarkably well during periods of stability.

The city itself did not need an enormous agricultural hinterland because its magical economy made international trade extraordinarily profitable. The College could export magical artifacts and specialized magical services in exchange for enormous quantities of mundane goods.

Surrounding civilizations therefore grew largely unimpeded.

The Precursor Mages had little interest in building an empire. Kingdoms and dynasties could rise and fall beyond their enormous gates while the College continued its research.

The College maintained territory beyond the city sufficient for some basic resources, but it remained heavily dependent upon international commerce.

That dependency would eventually expose the weakness of its political structure.


#### III. The Reagent Collector

Long before the political crisis, one particularly accomplished alchemist experienced the College's institutional dysfunction on a much smaller scale.

Over the course of his career, he accumulated an enormous personal collection of alchemical reagents.

Because the collection was exceptionally useful, he allowed other alchemists to borrow from it. Gradually, people stopped treating it as one scholar's private collection and began treating it as the College's reagent supply.

The collection became too large for him to administer casually.

He appealed to the College for help.

The College offered several unsatisfactory solutions.

At one point, mundane administrators were placed in charge. They could perform the administrative work but lacked the magical and alchemical expertise required to maintain the collection's quality.

Alternatively, the College could formally recognize the alchemist himself as responsible for the supply. But this effectively transformed a great scholar into a farmer and quartermaster. Instead of conducting his own research, he spent his time cultivating reagents and fulfilling requests from lesser alchemists.

The College expected him to provide an institutional service without providing the resources necessary to operate one.

Meanwhile, the alchemist discovered an exceptionally rare reagent growing in the mountains. Its habitat could not feasibly be reproduced at the College, and he believed research into it might become his magnum opus.

He began spending increasing amounts of time there.

First he made expeditions.

Then he moved a cot there for longer stays.

Then he built a shelter against the cold.

Days became weeks.

The mountain gradually became his real laboratory.

The final break came when the College officially reprimanded him for dereliction of his duties as quartermaster of the College alchemical supply.

He had never accepted such a position.

The reagents had originally been his own property.

After years of being expected to turn his private work into an uncompensated public service, the reprimand effectively ended his relationship with the College.

He remained in the mountains and became remembered as a hermit.


#### IV. The Great Crisis

Eventually, the geopolitical system supporting the College began to collapse.

Several foreign empires became embroiled in multi-front wars. These wars did not initially concern the College, but they devastated the trade networks upon which its enormous population depended.

Customers disappeared.

Shipments stopped arriving.

Ancient agriculture could not support a metropolis containing millions of people without enormous imports.

At the same time, the College's own hinterlands were subjected to sustained attacks by marauders and military deserters produced by the surrounding wars.

Infrastructure was destroyed. Trade became increasingly dangerous. Food and resources became scarce.

And the College discovered that its enormous bureaucracy had no effective mechanism for responding to a crisis that crossed administrative jurisdictions.

The mages themselves remained largely uninterested in government.

That vacuum was filled by others.


#### V. The Warlords and the Ultimatum

Powerful groups within the city began organizing their own militias.

These groups were diverse. They included trade unions, merchants, different administrative branches of government, and other affluent or influential organizations.

Initially, they were simply protecting their own interests.

As conditions deteriorated, however, they became increasingly political.

These militia leaders became the city's first real warlords.

Eventually, they confronted the College with an ultimatum.

They demanded three things:

Magical support in restoring the destroyed infrastructure.

The creation of an effective centralized authority.

Magical and material support for a military campaign against the marauders.

The threat was straightforward: if the College refused to help protect the population, the militias would eventually turn their weapons against the College itself.

For the first time, the College was forced to acknowledge that it was not merely an academic institution surrounded by support services.

It was responsible for a city.


#### VI. The Council of Thirteen

The emergency government was improvised.

Six representatives came from the mundane population and the emerging centers of power outside the College.

Six were College mages.

Neither side was willing to submit to the other, producing an obvious possibility of a six-to-six deadlock.

A thirteenth member was therefore required as a tie-breaker.

The thirteenth had to be a sufficiently senior mage to command the respect of the College, but they were required to live outside the College walls among ordinary citizens. The intention was to separate this person from petty College politics and force them to remain sensitive to the circumstances of the mundane population.

Finding such a person was difficult.

During the immediate crisis, someone remembered the ancient alchemist living in the mountains.

He was extraordinarily accomplished, unquestionably senior, and had been separated from College politics for decades.

The old hermit was brought back.

The administrative disaster confronting the city was, in many ways, the same disaster that had driven him away from the College—an informal system had accumulated obligations until nobody understood where authority and responsibility actually belonged.

He became the first thirteenth councillor.


#### VII. The Accidental Conquests

With centralized authority established, the militias and their magical support marched into the hinterlands.

They were not a professional army.

They were essentially an angry mob consisting of thousands of ordinary citizens, commanded by the city's existing power brokers and supported by College mages who had never really practiced warfare.

Initially, this hardly mattered.

The surrounding countryside contained marauders, deserters, remnants of armies, and other poorly organized armed groups. A local force might have a few hundred trained men-at-arms.

The College could send thousands of people.

More importantly, it could send mages.

College mages who were merely accomplished scholars by the standards of their peers were nearly incomprehensible forces to ordinary people. Many were able to unleash their magical abilities in warfare for the first time.

The militias smashed aside their opponents.

Then they reached the old boundaries of the College hinterlands.

And they kept going.

The borders themselves had never been particularly important or precisely defined. Years of war had made them even less meaningful.

Commanders attempted to secure their positions by eliminating nearby threats. Once one position was secured, another armed group beyond it represented a potential threat, so the militias advanced again.

They occupied what had effectively been no-man's-land for years.

At the same time, ambitious people recognized opportunities.

Military commanders could establish personal power bases beyond the city while still claiming the protection and legitimacy of the College. Mages could practice their abilities away from the restrictions of academic life.

The expansion snowballed.

Word reached the city quickly, but information was confused. Nobody was entirely certain where the legitimate border had been, which actions were defensive, or exactly what territory was now being claimed.

No one had consciously decided to create an empire.


#### VIII. The First Defeat

Eventually, one of these advancing forces crossed a border that was not ambiguous.

It entered the sovereign territory of an organized neighboring nation.

That state rallied a genuine army.

Its mages were nowhere near as broadly educated or individually sophisticated as those of the College. But they possessed something the College mages did not:

they were trained specifically for war.

The College's militia had learned terrible lessons from its string of effortless victories.

Its mages were extraordinarily powerful but inexperienced.

Its soldiers were numerous but poorly organized.

They now encountered disciplined troops supported by battle-mages who understood how to use their limited magical repertoire efficiently in combat.

The College force was defeated.

Its retreat became a rout.

The foreign army pursued them, killing thousands.

Several of the oldest and proudest College mages stood their ground, confident that their magical superiority could stop the enemy.

For once, their opponents did not simply break and disappear into the countryside.

The mages were overwhelmed and killed.


#### IX. The Peace

The victorious foreign general pursued the remnants of the College force into the no-man's-land.

He expected resistance.

None came.

There was no organized counterattack.

There were almost no meaningful fortifications.

Eventually, the general advanced so quickly that he outpaced his own supply lines and was forced to stop while they caught up.

He slowly realized something remarkable:

the College had almost no idea how to wage war.

Nevertheless, continuing the campaign presented little benefit.

Much of the disputed territory was near-useless land that had been effectively ungoverned before the College occupied it. Taking it would require continued campaigning while risking a permanent hostile relationship with some of the most individually powerful people in the world.

The foreign king therefore sued for peace.

The resulting treaty formally established borders.

Much of the former no-man's-land remained with the College.

For the first time, foreign diplomacy had effectively recognized the College as possessing a sizeable territorial state outside its original metropolis.


#### X. The Hermit Becomes a Ruler Without Realizing It

The peace treaty needed someone to sign it.

The College's logic was simple.

The six mundane and six magical representatives could deadlock. The thirteenth councillor existed to resolve such problems.

Therefore, the Hermit signed.

The College saw this as administrative convenience.

The outside world interpreted it very differently.

He signed the treaty. Therefore, he was in charge.

Foreign rulers began treating the thirteenth councillor as the de facto ruler of the College state.

The office gradually accumulated diplomatic significance without the College consciously creating a monarchy.


#### XI. Militarization

The humiliating defeat attracted professional soldiers and military scholars.

Mercenary companies realized that the College possessed enormous wealth, astonishing magical power, and virtually no institutional military knowledge.

They offered their services as instructors.

The College's response was characteristically academic:

It lacked expertise, so it hired experts.

The existing militias were not simply dissolved. A man already carrying a looted sword could be given a pay cheque to keep a road open. What began as another temporary measure gradually became permanent military service.

The armed forces grew primarily outside the College walls.

The College paid professional soldiers to teach its forces without seriously considering what the creation of a militarized society beyond its ivory tower might eventually mean.


#### XII. The Hermit's Retirement

The Hermit understood that his position was gradually transforming into something resembling kingship.

He did not particularly want it.

Once again, administrative responsibilities were consuming the time he wanted to devote to scholarship.

So, shortly after the crisis, he retired.

The College treated this as entirely normal.

They even threw him a retirement party.

The mundane councillors were horrified.

From their perspective, the ruler had just abdicated without a successor.

The thirteenth seat remained vacant.

Government became increasingly gridlocked. Foreign powers interpreted the vacancy as a succession crisis. Diplomatic relationships weakened.

Important trade agreements remained unsigned because the magical representatives wanted favorable rates on reagents while mundane representatives wanted favorable rates on commodities such as fine wines and oils.

Eventually, everyone had to admit that the thirteenth councillor was no longer merely a wartime expedient.

Functional government required the office.


#### XIII. The Early Tyrants

The thirteenth position became increasingly understood as an office sometimes referred to as the Tyrant, originally in the sense of an extraordinary individual authority created to resolve the failures of collective government.

Finding candidates remained difficult.

The position required a senior mage willing to leave the College behind.

The early government therefore cycled through several unusual candidates.

One was already on his deathbed and had little remaining academic career to sacrifice.

Another was a healer who could accept the requirement by moving permanently into a hospital outside the College and continuing to practice there.

Another was a theological convert who had come to believe that his magical power ultimately came from his faith.

The office therefore disproportionately attracted powerful misfits—mages who, for one reason or another, were willing to leave behind the academic life that most senior College mages valued above almost everything else.


#### XIV. The Discovery of Divine Power

The existence of religious mages eventually became intellectually significant.

Secular College scholars noticed that converts were different.

Whatever the theological explanation, there was observable evidence that devotion could provide access to a form of magical power.

The College began investigating.

Their conclusion was uncomfortable.

Divine power was real and, in some sense, available—but obtaining it required genuine devotion. A mage had to surrender substantial portions of their life to worship and religious obligations.

For scholars devoted above all else to research, this was an unattractive price.

Then they discovered another source.

Infernal Powers were willing to bargain.

They did not require the scholar to abandon research for worship.

Their power could be negotiated for.


#### XV. Centuries of Infernal Scholarship

Infernal research did not immediately destroy the College.

Quite the opposite.

It became a legitimate area of magical scholarship.

And for centuries, the predicted catastrophe failed to occur.

Religious authorities remained deeply uncomfortable with it. But generation after generation of College scholars could point to the evidence that they had successfully studied and bargained with Infernal Powers without opening Hell or destroying civilization.

The College came to believe that infernal magic could be understood, controlled, and safely incorporated into scholarship.

This continued for centuries.

The eventual collapse occurred only near the very end of Precursor history.


#### XVI. The Lich Project

One late branch of infernal research investigated lichdom.

For College scholars, immortality had an obvious intellectual attraction. Death destroyed centuries of accumulated expertise and ended research that might otherwise continue indefinitely.

The researchers initially attempted to solve the moral problems associated with lichdom.

They constructed a phylactery.

Physically, the object itself was not inherently evil. Without further magic, it was essentially a vessel capable of storing souls.

The problem was binding it to a living mage.

That required evil intent.

A relatively young scholar became the first successful subject.

He had reason to volunteer.

He suffered from an illness that was not immediately terminal, but ensured that he would never become an old man. He knew his research career would be far shorter than those of many of his peers.

He secretly discovered the true requirement for binding himself to the phylactery.

And then opportunity presented itself.


#### XVII. The First Stolen Soul

A mundane College worker—perhaps a janitor or gardener—fell from a ladder and was gravely injured.

The young scholar barely knew who this person was.

He could have helped.

Instead, he recognized an opportunity.

He cast the binding magic not to restrain the dying worker physically, but to condemn their soul to the vessel.

When the worker died, their soul was prevented from passing naturally into the afterlife.

That deliberate choice bound the phylactery to the scholar.

He then lied.

He told his colleagues that he had discovered a way to perform the binding without the required atrocity.

The College praised him for apparently overcoming the principal moral obstacle.

Almost nobody knew that the breakthrough was fraudulent.


#### XVIII. The Disturbance in the Firmament

The binding did not pass completely unnoticed.

Something about deliberately trapping the soul produced a disturbance in the divine order.

Only people exceptionally attuned to divine energies perceived it:

priests, saints, oracles, and similarly devout figures.

They could feel that something terrible had happened.

They did not necessarily know precisely what.

Then the College announced its breakthrough.

Religious authorities connected the two events.

The College denied that anything evil had occurred.

Most College scholars were telling the truth as they understood it. They believed the young scholar's account.

The controversy therefore became the word of the College against the word of religious authorities.

The general public had no definitive way to determine which side was correct.


#### XIX. Feeding the Phylactery

The researchers then began experimenting with how to sustain the bound scholar.

They wanted to find a method that avoided human suffering.

How many ants equaled a human soul?

How many rabbits?

Could lesser life provide enough energy?

When souls entered the phylactery, something remarkable happened.

The young scholar's failing health began improving.

But maintaining himself consumed the same energy the researchers were gathering.

He became trapped in a cycle:

harvest souls → consume their energy to preserve himself → expend more energy obtaining additional souls → harvest again.

His relationship with the phylactery increasingly resembled addiction.


#### XX. The Nature of the Soul Harvest

The effectiveness of a captured soul depended heavily upon its anguish at the moment of death.

The suffering remained preserved with it—final screams, terror, anger and desperation trapped inside the vessel.

Phylacteries did not simply vacuum up nearby souls.

Normally, the dead transitioned toward the afterlife.

But particularly anguished souls could remain oriented toward the mortal world, seeking to haunt or attack those responsible for their deaths.

A phylactery functioned almost like a dream catcher.

The victim's soul came looking for its killer.

The phylactery intercepted it.

This meant that both proximity and responsibility mattered.

If the scholar ordered a subordinate to poison someone, many of the victims' souls would seek the person who physically carried out the act.

Others would calm sufficiently to move naturally into the afterlife.

Only the most enraged or perceptive might continue up the chain of responsibility and seek the person who ordered their deaths.


#### XXI. The Cult

The young scholar gradually gathered a small group around himself.

These became essentially cultists.

They worked secretly to accelerate death without attracting attention.

Eventually, some of the inner members constructed phylacteries of their own.

This created a new problem.

Captured souls were now distributed between multiple participants.

If a subordinate committed a murder, their own phylactery could intercept the victims before those souls ever reached the young scholar who had ordered the killing.

The organization that allowed the scholar to scale his harvesting was now consuming the resource he needed.

His followers were also becoming rivals.

One of them might reach true lichdom before him.


#### XXII. The Rot Around the College

The cult therefore began causing suffering on increasingly large scales while attempting to make the resulting deaths appear natural.

They created plagues.

They manipulated weather to produce harvest failures.

They introduced substances into water supplies that harmed pregnancies and the unborn.

The purpose was to generate death, suffering and anguished souls without revealing that anyone was deliberately responsible.

To outsiders, however, a pattern gradually became visible.

It seemed as though a rot was emanating from the College.

The religious warnings that had accompanied the first phylactery binding suddenly looked far more credible.

The College had spent centuries insisting that its infernal research was controlled.

Now disease, famine, suffering and strange calamities seemed to follow in its wake.

Most of the College still did not know what the cult was actually doing.


#### XXIII. The Scholar's Final Problem

The young scholar eventually recognized that indirect killing could never give him what he needed.

Delegation diluted responsibility.

Distance allowed souls to calm and pass onward.

Cultists with their own phylacteries intercepted victims who blamed them directly.

And every cultist who advanced became a potential rival.

He needed an atrocity of enormous scale for which he personally bore responsibility.

He also needed to act before one of his followers surpassed or usurped him.

He had access to millions of potential victims living in an extraordinarily concentrated metropolis.

And centuries of infernal scholarship had taught him exactly what demons would do if given unrestricted access to mortals.


#### XXIV. The Portal

The scholar deliberately arranged what appeared to be an accident.

In reality, he opened a portal to Hell.

His intention was to unleash demons upon the metropolis.

The resulting suffering would produce an unprecedented quantity of anguished souls, and because he had personally opened the portal, he expected enough of those souls ultimately to recognize him as responsible.

The College's centuries of confidence in infernal research now collapsed in a single catastrophe.

The rot people had feared was no longer theoretical.

Hell had been opened.


#### XXV. The Final Tyrant

By this period, the office of Tyrant had long required its holder to leave the College and live among ordinary people.

The arrangement had originally been intended to protect the government from College politics and ensure that its most powerful arbiter remained aware of mundane concerns.

Now that separation became a fatal weakness.

The Tyrant did not know what was really happening with the phylactery.

He knew that something terrible had occurred within the College and understood the magnitude of the infernal crisis, but he did not know that a young scholar was already bound to a soul vessel and approaching lichdom.

The Tyrant himself was a religious man.

Faced with the portal and the possibility of Hell spreading beyond the metropolis, he reached an appalling conclusion.

The city could not remain where it was.


#### XXVI. The Teleportation

Around the equivalent of 1000 BC, the Tyrant used magic to teleport the entire Precursor metropolis to a distant continent.

He intended to remove the infernal catastrophe from the world and prevent anyone from continuing the College's work.

The destination was catastrophic.

The enormous city materialized inside a mountain.

Millions died.

Buildings intersected stone. People were crushed, buried, suffocated, or trapped in voids within the mountain.

The Tyrant died instantly with them.

But the action succeeded in its immediate purpose.

The portal was removed.

The College disappeared.

Its infernal research vanished with it.

The outside world was spared an invasion from Hell.


#### XXVII. The Dead Seek Their Killer

The deaths created an unimaginable wave of anguish.

Many souls sought the person responsible for their deaths.

That person was, immediately, the Tyrant.

But the Tyrant had acted without selfish intent. He had sacrificed himself alongside his people to prevent a vastly greater catastrophe.

He was also a faithful man.

The divine powers understood the magnitude and nature of his choice and protected his soul.

The anguished dead could not attach themselves to him.

Many eventually moved naturally onward.

Others remained restless.

But some continued searching through the chain of responsibility.

Why had the Tyrant killed them?

Because the city had to be removed.

Why had it needed to be removed?

Because Hell had been opened.

And who had opened Hell?

The young scholar.


#### XXVIII. The First Lich

Unlike the Tyrant, the young scholar survived the immediate teleportation.

By extraordinary chance, the portion of the city containing him materialized within a void inside the mountain rather than solid rock.

He remained alive.

He remained bound to his phylactery.

And unlike the Tyrant, there was no divine protection surrounding him.

He had spent years steeped in infernal corruption.

He had deliberately created the catastrophe.

He was responsible for the chain of events that had caused millions of deaths.

To the lost and enraged dead searching for someone to blame, he became a beacon.

Not every soul reached him.

Most did not need to.

Enough did.

His phylactery caught them.

Thousands upon thousands of final screams, gasps, terror and anguish flooded into the vessel.

The event that had been intended to destroy the College and save the world accidentally supplied the young scholar with what his research had never managed to achieve.

The teleportation created the lich.


#### XXIX. The Missing City

Back on the original continent, the disappearance left an enormous scar upon the earth.

Thousands of years later, the land remains visibly desolate.

The metropolis itself is gone.

By the equivalent of the campaign's 1600s/1700s, the Precursor Mages are known largely through their absence, scattered artifacts, religious traditions, fragmentary history, and the impossible wasteland where one of the greatest cities in the ancient world once stood.

The truth is buried on another continent.

Inside a mountain lies the lost metropolis: its buildings, College, infernal research, dead inhabitants, and the lich created during its destruction.

For roughly two and a half millennia, the Tyrant's solution has held.

The world survived.

The College's knowledge remained inaccessible.

The lich remained entombed.

And then, in the era of the campaign, people begin searching for a legendary lost city—an El Dorado—without understanding why that city was deliberately removed from the world in the first place.


### The Arrival (how the natives react)

It appeared inside the central mountain range during an ancient magical catastrophe—apparently the result of a failed teleportation on an unimaginable scale.

Its arrival is remembered as a world-changing event.

The catastrophe may have produced earthquakes, landslides, unnatural storms, strange lights, magical contamination, mass animal deaths, and entire valleys being reshaped.

Different traditions call the event the Falling City, the Arrival, or the Shattering.

No surviving native culture necessarily understands that this was a teleportation accident.

To them, it may have been divine punishment, a fallen heaven, an invasion from another reality, or the arrival of false gods.

The mountain range became taboo specifically because of the city.

Most native peoples avoid the central mountains unless absolutely necessary.

Certain trails are deliberately destroyed, disguised, or omitted from oral maps.

Old stone markers warn travelers away.

Children are taught never to follow lights into the mountains or enter caves containing carved stone.

Travelers returning from the region may be ritually cleansed, isolated, or treated with suspicion.


### The Nhal’Kesh — Containment

The Nhal’Kesh are the people most directly tied to containing the city.

Their ancestors may have witnessed its arrival.

They believe the city is imprisoned inside the mountain and that something within it is still alive.

Their rituals exist partly to prevent that presence—or the city itself—from escaping.

Their blood rites reinforce magical seals they no longer fully understand.

Certain wards around the mountain require regular ritual maintenance.

Sacrifice may provide the power needed to renew them.

Outsiders see horrifying ceremonies and assume primitive superstition; some of those ceremonies genuinely work.

Forbidden temples near mountain entrances are actually watchposts.

Priest-warriors monitor magical disturbances, strange creatures, earthquakes, lights from the mountain, and disappearances.

They kill or drive off treasure hunters because every expedition risks breaking a seal or disturbing something.

This makes them natural enemies of colonial archaeologists, prospectors, and artifact hunters.

Their mythology preserves distorted memories of the mages.

The city’s inhabitants are remembered as star-devils, false gods, fire kings, or people who tore holes in the sky.

Some ceremonial masks may imitate ancient robes, helmets, or magical apparatus without anyone remembering their original purpose.

Of the major cultures, the Nhal’Kesh may be closest to understanding the immediate danger.


### The Aelari — The Wounded Land

The Aelari understand the city as an ecological and spiritual infection.

Trees twist near contaminated ground.

Animals avoid certain valleys.

Rivers change color near underground passages.

Seasons behave incorrectly, and certain flowers bloom only around magical contamination.

They believe the mountain is alive, wounded, and trying to heal around a foreign object buried inside it.

Their shamans can sense the city indirectly through dreams, animal behavior, migration, and strange rainfall patterns.

The closer someone travels to the city, the stronger these disturbances become.

They maintain a broad exclusion zone without obvious walls or armies.

Controlled burns, misleading trails, sacred groves, and carefully placed settlements redirect travelers away from the mountains.

They sometimes rescue people who escape from the city.

Survivors may emerge starving, hallucinating, cursed, diseased, or magically contaminated.

Aelari purification practices are dismissed by outsiders until those practices prove effective.

Their position is absolute: the city should not be entered, studied, or preserved. It should be allowed to disappear beneath the earth.

This places them directly at odds with scholars who believe understanding the city could revolutionize magic or science.

### Entrances, Routes, and Native Knowledge

Nobody knows a single, obvious main entrance.

There are dozens of supposed entrances: collapsed tunnels, volcanic caves, ancient stairways, underground rivers, sealed doors, and sinkholes exposing pieces of architecture.

Most lead nowhere. Some reach only peripheral structures. A handful connect to the actual city.

Different peoples hold different pieces of the route.

The Nhal’Kesh can identify active seals, watched entrances, and signs that a passage should never be opened.

The Aelari can navigate the exclusion zone, recognize contamination, and purify some survivors.

The Canopy People know elevated mountain approaches and oral route-songs preserved across generations.

The Flesh-Shapers know contaminated shortcuts and may possess specimens or fragments taken from the outskirts.

No single faction can provide everything an expedition needs, making diplomacy part of reaching the dungeon rather than merely background lore.


### Colonial Discovery and Campaign Escalation

Credible evidence of the city transforms the colonial race.

The island stops being merely rich and becomes potentially the most strategically important place in the world.

Companies begin hunting lost magical technology, teleportation research, weapons, immortality magic, ancient spellbooks, manufacturing methods, and artifacts capable of changing the balance of power between nations.


### Campaign Structure****

The party serves as the governing council of their chartered expedition.

Each character oversees a major sector such as Defense, Economy, Tribal Affairs, Exploration, Logistics, Research, or Infrastructure.

As the expedition grows, their responsibilities expand from managing a small foothold to directing a full colonial operation.

The council’s decisions shape the colony, its relationships, and the challenges the party faces.


#### Phase 1 — Exploration and Colonization

Establish the first foothold on the island.

Explore the coastline and nearby jungle.

Identify resources, routes, and potential settlement locations.

Begin encountering rival companies and native populations.

Introduce the council structure and the responsibilities of each player.


#### Phase 2 — Native Politics and Territorial Conflict

Expansion begins to overlap with native territory.

The party navigates diplomacy, alliances, disputes, and cultural tensions.

Rival companies begin competing for native support and strategic land.

Council decisions begin having lasting effects on the political landscape.


#### Phase 3 — Evidence of the Mage City

Credible evidence emerges that an enormous ancient mage city lies within the central mountain range.

The discovery shifts the priorities of the expedition.

Rival companies begin actively searching for the city and attempting to steal or suppress information.

The council must decide how aggressively to pursue the discovery and how much information to reveal.


#### Phase 4 — The Race for the Mountains

Competition between the chartered companies escalates into open conflict, espionage, sabotage, and proxy warfare.

Every faction attempts to secure routes, resources, alliances, and strategic positions near the mountains.

The council must balance military, political, economic, and logistical priorities as the race intensifies.


#### Phase 5 — Establishing the Forward Operating Base

The party establishes a permanent FOB near the mage city.

The campaign shifts more heavily into a Kingmaker-style management phase.

Each council member develops and manages their sector of the settlement.

The party must secure resources, maintain supply lines, strengthen alliances, defend the base, and prepare for long-term exploration of the city.

The condition of the FOB directly affects how effectively the party can operate within the dungeon.


#### Phase 6 — The Great Expedition

The large-scale exploration of the buried mage city begins.

The dungeon is explored gradually through repeated expeditions rather than as a single continuous crawl.

The party returns to the FOB between expeditions to recover, resupply, develop the settlement, and respond to events in the outside world.

Rival companies may establish their own bases or enter the city through alternate routes.

Progress within the dungeon and the strength of the colony become increasingly dependent on one another.


### Core Campaign Loop

Govern the colony → explore the island → manage alliances and rivalries → strengthen the FOB → enter the mage city → return with resources and information → adapt the colony → push deeper.

The campaign should maintain a balance between exploration, political decision-making, faction conflict, settlement development, and dungeon crawling, with each part of the game influencing the others.


### Established Facts About the Precursor City


### Origin: The civilization began around 2000 BC as an apolitical College of Mages rather than a kingdom or planned city.


### Growth: The metropolis accumulated around the College as mundane workers, merchants, builders, and families took over the practical work scholars delegated.


### Population: At its height, millions lived around the College, and most inhabitants were not mages.


### Government: A fragmented academic bureaucracy gave way to the Council of Thirteen and eventually the office of the Tyrant.


### Territory: Emergency campaigns against marauders expanded unintentionally into a recognized territorial state.


### Magical culture: The College treated magic as scholarship and eventually normalized bargains with Infernal Powers after centuries of apparently controlled research.


### The lich project: A dying young scholar secretly trapped a worker’s soul, founded a hidden cult, and engineered widespread suffering to feed his phylactery.


### The catastrophe: The scholar deliberately opened a portal to Hell so that the deaths of the metropolis would complete his transformation.


### The teleportation: Around 1000 BC, the Final Tyrant moved the entire metropolis to another continent to remove the portal and prevent a wider infernal invasion.


### The Arrival: The city materialized inside the island’s central mountain, killing millions and fusing entire districts with solid rock.


### Current state: The College, its infernal research, the dead metropolis, and the first lich remain entombed beneath the mountain.

### Time Period and Technology

The setting roughly resembles the late Age of Sail transitioning toward the early industrial era.

A visual and technological reference point would be roughly the late 1700s through early-to-mid 1800s, without needing to precisely mirror real-world history.

Sailing ships still dominate long-distance travel.

Muskets, rifles, pistols, cannons, and blades coexist.

Professional soldiers fight alongside mercenaries, explorers, sailors, hunters, and company militias.

Printing presses, universities, scientific societies, banks, and international trade networks are well established.

Early industrial technology is beginning to change the world but has not yet completely transformed it.

Technology should feel capable but limited.

Expeditions can bring sophisticated equipment, firearms, medicine, surveying tools, and engineering knowledge.

However, the jungle remains extremely difficult to tame.

Roads must be constructed.

Supplies must be transported manually or by animals and boats.

Communication with distant settlements may take days or weeks.

Reinforcements from the Old World may take weeks/months to arrive.

A company can possess impressive technology while still being completely vulnerable hundreds of miles into unexplored rainforest.

Firearms are common but do not make traditional weapons obsolete.

Firearms are powerful, especially in organized military formations.

They are also noisy, dependent on ammunition, vulnerable to moisture, and difficult to resupply deep in the jungle.

Bows, spears, blades, traps, poisons, and ambush tactics remain extremely effective.

Native forces familiar with the terrain can still be extraordinarily dangerous to technologically superior expeditionary armies.

Industrialization is beginning to emerge.

Steam technology may exist but remains relatively expensive and uncommon.

Steamships could be beginning to appear on major rivers and international routes.

Primitive rail systems, pumps, mechanical excavation equipment, or industrial machinery may exist in wealthy colonial settlements later in the campaign.

Establishing this infrastructure on the island should represent a major achievement rather than something immediately available.


### Magic Level

Magic exists openly, but it is uncommon enough to remain important.

The setting is neither low-magic nor traditional high-fantasy.

Most ordinary people know that magic exists, but relatively few individuals can actually wield significant magical power.

A trained mage is comparable to a skilled physician, engineer, scholar, or military specialist: valuable, educated, and relatively rare.

Magic has become partially institutionalized in the established world.

Universities and academies study it.

Governments employ court mages and military specialists.

Companies hire magical consultants for dangerous expeditions.

Scholars attempt to classify magical phenomena scientifically.

Magic is increasingly treated as something that can be studied and systematized rather than purely mystical.

Magic has limitations that prevent it from solving the logistical challenges of the setting.

Teleportation is exceptionally difficult, dangerous, rare, or heavily restricted.

Long-distance communication magic is limited.

Healing magic can save lives but cannot eliminate disease, injury, or the need for medicine.

Creating food and water magically cannot sustain entire colonial populations.

Magical transportation cannot replace roads, ships, caravans, and supply networks.

These limitations preserve the exploration and Kingmaker elements of the campaign.

Different cultures understand magic differently.

Foreign nations increasingly approach it through formal education, experimentation, and academic theory.

Native traditions may interpret the same forces through spirituality, ritual, oral tradition, ancestral knowledge, or relationships with the natural world.

Neither approach necessarily represents the complete truth.

The island possesses significantly more magical activity than the outside world.

Strange plants, creatures, ruins, minerals, and environmental phenomena occur with unusual frequency.

This becomes another major reason foreign powers are interested in the island.

Scholars suspect that something about the island concentrates, produces, or attracts magical energy.


### Ancient Magic

Modern magic is considerably less powerful than the magic of the ancient world.

The buried mage city represents a level of magical knowledge that modern civilizations cannot easily reproduce.

Ancient mages accomplished things considered nearly impossible by contemporary standards.

Their artifacts, infrastructure, architecture, and magical systems therefore have enormous historical and strategic value.

The teleportation disaster that buried the mage city demonstrates the scale of ancient magic.

An entire city was transported across an enormous distance and embedded within the central mountain range.

Whether this happened intentionally, accidentally, or as part of a larger catastrophe remains unclear.

The event permanently altered the surrounding region and became incorporated into native mythology.

Ancient magical artifacts could potentially transform the balance of power in the outside world.

This is why discovering the mage city becomes much more significant than simply finding treasure.

A company that successfully recovers and understands ancient magical technology could give its sponsoring nation an enormous advantage over its rivals.


### Tone of the Setting

The world should feel like a mixture of exploration, colonial competition, political intrigue, wilderness survival, and ancient fantasy.

Early portions of the campaign emphasize discovery and the excitement of entering a largely unknown world.

As settlements expand, the political and moral consequences of colonization become increasingly apparent.

Rival companies become more aggressive.

Native alliances and conflicts become increasingly important.

The discovery of the mage city gradually introduces a stronger element of ancient mystery and high fantasy.

The setting should avoid clearly dividing factions into heroes and villains.

Some companies genuinely value discovery, trade, and scientific advancement.

Others are ruthlessly exploitative.

Native civilizations have their own rivalries, ambitions, traditions, and internal divisions.

Individual council decisions can create allies or enemies regardless of faction.

The contrast between the familiar and the unknown is important.

The coastal colonies feel increasingly organized, industrialized, and recognizable.

Traveling inland means gradually leaving that world behind.

Deep jungle expeditions feel increasingly isolated.

The mountains feel ancient and forbidden.

The buried mage city represents the point where the assumptions of the modern world begin to completely break down.


#### General Setting Feel

Age of Sail colonial frontier + early industrial expansion + corporate proxy warfare + dense tropical exploration + moderate institutional magic + remnants of an ancient high-magic civilization.
